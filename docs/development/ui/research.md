# Research

Published XR guidance, interaction toolkits, authoring frameworks, layout and text libraries, and other VR shells inform the toolkit's constraints. Comparisons describe design rationale rather than a guarantee that every referenced mechanism exists. Shared claims and manipulation remain deferred in [#69](https://github.com/philpax/lunette/issues/69), pick hooks and proximity queries in [#70](https://github.com/philpax/lunette/issues/70), and maintained arrangements in [#71](https://github.com/philpax/lunette/issues/71).

## XR design guidance

Published guidance gives the numbers behind the default tokens in [Theming](theming.md). Most of it is angular. At 1 metre, 1 degree is 17.5 millimetres, and one distance-independent millimetre (dmm) is one millimetre seen at one metre.

[Microsoft's interactable object guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/mixed-reality/design/interactable-object) gives a minimum of 1 degree for gaze and hand-ray targets, stated at 2 metres, and 2 degrees at 45 cm for direct touch, which is about 16 mm. Its [typography guidance](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/mixed-reality/design/typography) gives 0.35 to 0.4 degrees as the minimum legible text height and 0.6 to 0.75 degrees as comfortable at 2 metres, and 0.4 to 0.5 and 0.65 to 0.8 degrees at 45 cm. It notes that thin strokes vibrate at small sizes and recommends at most two families. The toolkit takes the text sizes and the weight and family rules. The 1 degree ray minimum is a floor rather than a default, because other guidance asks for much larger targets.

Meta's [hand interaction guidance](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/design/hands-ui-best-practices/) gives roughly 2.5 to 3 degrees for both direct touch and ray targets, and stresses that bare hands have no haptics, so depth and contact need visual cues. Its [raycasting specification](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/design/raycasting_specs/) defines cursor behaviour: a constant angular cursor size, a select ring about 83 percent of the hover ring, hover and press transition timings, and a tiebreak for surfaces within one centimetre of each other along the ray. Its [Android app design requirements](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/android-apps/design-requirements) ask for 48 dp targets and 60 dp for primary controls, use invisible hit slop, and recommend light backgrounds no brighter than #DADADA. These inform target sizing, cursor design, hand feedback, and the rule against pure white. They do not establish automatic hit-slop expansion in the current host.

[Android XR's visual design guidance](https://developer.android.com/design/ui/xr/guides/visual-design) defines 1 dp as 0.868 dmm, and recommends 56 dp targets around a 48 dp visual affordance; the 48 dp minimum comes from Material Design. Its [spatial UI guidance](https://developer.android.com/design/ui/xr/guides/spatial-ui) defines elevation levels for orbiters, popups, and dialogs at 16, 32, and 56 dp in its table, while its prose gives orbiters 15 dp, warns that the values may change, and advises against elevating large or scrollable areas. The toolkit takes the dialog elevation, the rule against elevating scrollable areas, and orbiters as the model for attached panels at a panel's edge. It does not take the popup elevation: seen from the side, a menu 28 mm above its dropdown looks detached from it, so overlays stand one control step above their opener instead.

Apple's visionOS Human Interface Guidelines and the WWDC23 session [Design for spatial user interfaces](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10076/) ask for at least 60 points of space for each interactive target, and show hover effects on interactive elements, with a custom hover shape where an element's shape is not a simple rounded rectangle. The WWDC24 session [Dive deep into volumes and immersive spaces](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2024/10153/) describes windows that scale up to keep their size in the field of view as they move away. The WWDC23 session [Create accessible spatial experiences](https://developer.apple.com/videos/play/wwdc2023/10034/) treats reduced motion, larger text, and alternatives to head-anchored content as system settings that applications read. These inform hover response and accessibility settings. Keeping world panels at a fixed angular size is a possible placement policy, not a current host option. Points have no fixed physical size, so the 60 point figure is not converted.

## Ray pointing research

Pressing a trigger moves the controller and the ray with it. In a CHI 2020 late-breaking work, Wolf and colleagues ([PDF](https://www.uni-ulm.de/fileadmin/website_uni_ulm/iui.inst.100/institut/mitarbeiterbereiche/wolf/HeisenbergCHI20.pdf)) call this movement the Heisenberg effect. It causes 30.45 percent of selection errors overall. In their ballistic condition, where the user is still moving towards the target when pressing, it causes 30.49 percent of errors; in their stationary condition, 81.98 percent. A correction that subtracts offsets averaged per posture and degree of freedom, pooled across participants, reduces Heisenberg errors by 25.4 percent; a single averaged offset reduces them by about 19.5 percent. They also test shifting the selection back in time to the start of the press. In the ballistic condition that was harmful: 55.7 percent of initial presses were already outside the target, against 7 percent in the stationary condition, and for the largest targets the error rate rose from 26.6 to 37.4 percent. Time-shifting helped only in the stationary condition.

The laser's trigger lock holds a sufficiently steady ray's preceding pose while a press begins and unlocks after deliberate movement exceeds its configured limits. [Input](input.md#ray-robustness) links the provider that owns those thresholds. A stricter stationarity test over a longer window and device-specific offset correction remain options if headset testing shows mis-selections. The published result makes moving presses an important test case rather than establishing that any time-shift is beneficial.

## Interaction toolkits

### StereoKit

[StereoKit](https://github.com/StereoKit/StereoKit) is an open-source XR engine with an immediate-mode UI. A window is declared every frame with `UI.WindowBegin` and `UI.WindowEnd`, widgets are calls between them, and ids are hashes of an id stack combined with each widget's label. Layout is cursor-based in the manner of Dear ImGui, with `ui_layout_*` functions such as `SameLine`, `LayoutReserve`, and `LayoutPushCut`, and windows size to their content using the previous frame's extent. `UI.PushSurface` places subsequent widgets on an arbitrary pose.

Its development branch models input as interactors. An interactor is a point, such as a fingertip, or a line, such as a ray, hit-tested as a capsule, with an event mask for poke, pinch, and grip, an activation type that is either a button state or a physical position, a source mask that keeps one hand's interactors from competing, and a minimum distance that keeps far rays from acting on elements within reach. Elements register their volumes every frame, and the nearest element by priority and distance takes focus. Buttons have 10 mm of depth and activate at half of it, and pressed geometry follows the finger. Theming uses settings for margin, padding, gutter, depth, and rounding, colour categories with normal, active, and disabled states, and about 20 visual slots whose mesh, material, and sound can each be replaced. Its quadrant meshes resize like a three-dimensional nine-slice, so bevels keep their shape at any size.

StereoKit informs physical press depth, per-hand near-versus-far behavior, and replaceable [visuals](extension.md#visuals). Open interactor shapes and a general equivalent of `PushSurface` remain design ideas rather than public host mechanisms. Immediate mode, label-derived ids, cursor-based layout, and previous-frame sizing suit small tools but not dense retained interfaces. StereoKit scrolls text but has no general scroll container, flexbox, grid, or focus traversal beyond text input.

### Meta Interaction SDK

The [Meta Interaction SDK](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/unity/unity-isdk-poke-interaction/), with details from its API reference, pairs interactors and interactables by type: poke, ray, and grab. Both feed one pointer event stream of hover, unhover, select, unselect, move, and cancel. [Interactor groups](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/unity/unity-isdk-interactor-group/) arbitrate within one hand: the default group lets one interactor hover and select at a time, and another lets several hover while one selects. Poke interaction defines a hover zone in front of the surface with hysteresis and a minimum approach along the normal, separate drag thresholds along and across the surface, a cancel distance, and a recoil assist that allows fast repeated presses. Its default selection method tests the fingertip crossing the surface, so a poke must approach from the front. A limiter clamps the rendered hand at the surface so the finger does not appear to pass through.

The toolkit takes the front-approach rule, hysteresis, recoil, re-arming, and per-hand near reporting described in [Input](input.md#near-touch). Its parameter values are its own tuning choices, not values from the SDK. A rendered contact cue does not establish the SDK's complete hand limiter. Nodes receive semantic events instead of typed pairs of interactors and interactables.

### MRTK3

[MRTK3](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/mixed-reality/mrtk-unity/mrtk3-overview/architecture/interactors) builds on Unity's XR Interaction Toolkit. Interactables respond to generic hovered and selected states, not to interactor types. Each interactor exposes an attach transform for manipulation and a variable select progress for analog triggers and pinches. An [interaction mode manager](https://learn.microsoft.com/en-us/windows/mixed-reality/mrtk-unity/mrtk3-input/packages/input/interaction-mode-manager) enables subsets of interactors together, such as disabling rays when a hand is near a surface. Solvers such as RadialView, Follow, HandConstraint, and Orbital place objects relative to the user, a hand, or another object, and chain together.

MRTK informs semantic node events and the near-versus-far rule. A session-level mode service would support the [input-mode policy](presentations.md#input-modes), but the current contracts do not provide one. Attach poses and solvers inform deferred manipulation and possible placement extensions; the current host has closed presentation policies rather than a solver registry.

### visionOS and Android XR

In visionOS, the system renders gaze hover outside the application's process, and controls receive the same gestures from gaze and pinch, direct touch, and a trackpad pointer. Ornaments attach views outside a window's edge, and RealityView attachments place two-dimensional views as entities in a scene. Android XR's Jetpack Compose for XR offers orbiters at a panel's content edge with an offset and alignment, and spatial panels that can be moved and resized. These systems inform semantic events and attached panel chrome. They do not establish a general node-anchor or `attach` placement API in the host. Gaze remains open in [Input](input.md#open-questions).

## Desktop and web UI frameworks

Wayland's `xdg_popup` and `xdg_positioner` place a popup relative to an anchor rectangle on its parent surface, with an anchor edge, a gravity, an offset, and constraint adjustment that flips, slides, or resizes the popup when it would leave the output. An explicit grab sends input to the popup chain, and the compositor may dismiss the chain on a press outside it. Separate toolkit overlay panels use the same general rationale. They implement a smaller placement contract, without a body-derived comfortable-field or reach constraint region, as [Presentations](presentations.md#portals-and-overlays) describes.

Flutter resolves competing gestures in a gesture arena, in which recognisers on the hit path claim a pointer and the arena declares one winner. UIKit's gesture recognisers express the same with failure requirements. The web's DOM routes events through capture and bubble phases. Those are models for the deferred competing claims in [#69](https://github.com/philpax/lunette/issues/69). The current host bubbles events and starts a declared drag on the captured node; it has no general capture-phase handler or gesture arena.

Spring-based animation libraries, such as react-spring, Framer Motion, and SwiftUI's default animations, retarget a spring mid-flight while keeping its velocity, which keeps rapid hover changes smooth. Framer Motion's `AnimatePresence` keeps a removed element alive for its exit animation. The toolkit takes both, as [Motion and feedback](motion-and-feedback.md) describes.

Android's `ExploreByTouchHelper` exposes virtual views inside a custom view to accessibility services. It is a model for addressable objects within a custom canvas. The host currently exposes nodes rather than a general item registry.

## Visual design

The default theme borrows from two places outside XR. Windows' Metro design language, and the Zune and Windows Phone interfaces it came from, carried its identity in confident type and flat blocks of colour rather than in decoration; the toolkit takes header bands that fill their row and hierarchy through type. Physical instruments and hardware, with matte faces, visible edges, keys that travel, and engraved values with units, are the model for controls that read as objects in a headset. Both are deliberate departures from the tonal surfaces and state layers that mark Material Design, which Android XR inherits.

## Authoring models

[React](https://react.dev/) and its Luau port [react-lua](https://github.com/jsdotlua/react-lua) model an interface as function components that return elements, with hooks for state and effects, reconciled by key against the previous output. The toolkit takes this model, because plain Lua control flow inside a component is the easiest model for plugin authors, and because a keyed reconciler is what hand-written retained UI in Lua converges on. It leaves out react-lua itself: its fibre architecture, scheduler, and full hook set are larger than the toolkit needs, and its allocation per render is a cost in Luau.

[Vide](https://github.com/centau/vide), whose reactive primitive is called a source, and [Fusion](https://github.com/dphfox/Fusion) are signal libraries for Luau. A component runs once and binds reactive closures to instance properties, so updates are precise and no diffing happens. Fusion 0.3 introduces scopes that own cleanup, which resemble the plugin runtime's owners. The toolkit takes observable stores for service state, and scoping of effects to owners. It leaves out signals as the standard authoring model, because conditional content and lists need special helpers and a read outside a reactive closure loses its reactivity silently. [Authoring](authoring.md#comparison-with-other-models) records the comparison.

[Iris](https://github.com/SirMallard/Iris) offers a Dear ImGui-style immediate API over retained Roblox instances, keyed by call site. [Plasma](https://github.com/matter-ecs/plasma) offers immediate-mode calls with React-like hooks. Both show that an immediate-looking API can sit on retained nodes. The toolkit takes the separation between authoring and retained nodes, which is what allows other frameworks to target the same host, as [Extension](extension.md#other-frameworks) describes.

[Stardust XR's asteroids](https://github.com/StardustXR/asteroids) is a declarative layer, loosely in the style of Elm: application state produces an element tree that is diffed against the previous one, and events are closures that change the state. It is a precedent for declarative UI in a VR shell.

## LÖVR UI libraries

[lovr-ui](https://github.com/immortalx74/lovr-ui) is an immediate-mode library for LÖVR in the style of Dear ImGui, with laser pointers, thumbstick list scrolling, and an on-screen keyboard. [chui](https://github.com/jmiskovic/chui) is a retained library with pressable three-dimensional widgets, row layout inside panels, and interaction from controllers, fingertips, or the mouse. Both show what LÖVR's drawing primitives support directly. The toolkit takes chui's pressable widgets and its use of the same widgets for touch and rays. Neither library has the layout, text, extension, or ownership model the session needs.

## Layout engines

[taffy](https://github.com/DioxusLabs/taffy) is a Rust implementation of CSS block, flexbox, and grid layout, including right-to-left direction, with a per-node cache that makes relayout incremental. It offers a ready-made tree and a low-level API of traits that a custom tree implements, which lets a custom tree add its own layout algorithms alongside taffy's. It is used by Bevy, Servo, Slint, Zed, and WayVR's wgui. Its rounding to whole units is on by default and is disabled for layout in metres. Its 0.x releases change its API. The toolkit uses its low-level API, as [Layout and text](layout-and-text.md#native-binding) describes.

[Yoga](https://github.com/facebook/yoga) is the C++ flexbox engine behind React Native. It is mature, but its grid support is limited to a style API without a layout algorithm, and it adds a C++ build dependency to a Rust host.

[Clay](https://github.com/nicbarker/clay) is a single-header C library that lays out an immediate-mode tree redeclared every frame, with fit, grow, fixed, and percentage sizing, scroll containers, and a sorted list of render commands. It is fast, but redeclaring the tree every frame from Luau would be one native call per node per frame, and it has no grid.

[morphorm](https://github.com/vizia/morphorm) is the layout engine of the vizia toolkit, with a smaller feature set than flexbox. It offers no advantage over taffy for this use.

## Text

[cosmic-text](https://github.com/pop-os/cosmic-text) provides shaping through harfrust, a Rust port of HarfBuzz, bidirectional text, font fallback through fontdb, line wrapping, ellipsis with a line limit, and an editor with caret and selection. It reads metrics through skrifa, which it re-exports, and rasterises through swash, and is used by the COSMIC desktop and WayVR's wgui. The toolkit reads outlines through skrifa directly, because it generates distance fields rather than bitmaps. [parley](https://github.com/linebender/parley) provides shaping, fallback through fontique, and a rich styled-span model, and is the text layer of the Linebender stack. [HarfBuzz](https://harfbuzz.github.io/) is the reference shaping engine in C. The toolkit uses cosmic-text, as [Layout and text](layout-and-text.md#shaping-and-wrapping) describes.

Multi-channel signed distance field text keeps glyphs sharp across distances and scales, which suits world-space panels. LÖVR already renders text this way, with msdfgen. Rounded corners at very small sizes and the absence of hinting remain limits. [Theming](theming.md#typography) treats legible size as a design constraint; the host does not enforce an angular minimum that removes those limits.

## Other VR desktop shells

WayVR's [wgui](https://wayvr.org/docs/wgui/) is a retained-mode Vulkan GUI library built on taffy, cosmic-text, and resvg, as its [source](https://github.com/wayvr-org/wayvr) shows. Interfaces are declared in XML with templates, macros, and includes. Its elements are boxes, labels, rectangles, sprites, and images with flex, block, and grid attributes, and its components include buttons, sliders, checkboxes, tabs, edit boxes, radio groups, colour selectors, and tooltips. Theme colours are named tokens with opacity variants. WayVR is lunette's closest relative, and its compositor code is vendored in this repository. The toolkit takes wgui's choice of taffy and cosmic-text and its token naming approach. It leaves out XML layout, because components in Lua compose with plugin state directly, and Rust-side widgets, because widgets in lunette are plugin policy.

The [SteamVR dashboard](https://developer.valvesoftware.com/wiki/Panorama) uses Valve's Panorama framework, with XML panels, a CSS dialect, and JavaScript, rendered to overlay textures. It shows the reach of a web-like layout and styling model in VR. It renders flat textures, which the depth requirement rules out.

[Stardust XR](https://github.com/StardustXR) is a Rust VR shell whose clients connect to a server that handles input through signed distance fields. Its [molecules](https://github.com/StardustXR/molecules) library provides widgets. xrdesktop and SimulaVR present desktop windows as textured quads and have little native UI of their own.

## Rendering strategies

Rendering panels to cached textures and submitting them as [composition layers](https://developers.meta.com/horizon/documentation/native/android/os-compositor-layers/) reduces resampling. Drawing geometry in the scene preserves per-element depth and depth testing against toolkit rays and proxies. The toolkit uses scene geometry and Luau-packed drawing data, as [Rendering](rendering.md) explains. Persistent native instance slots, a separate animation buffer, and shader-evaluated proximity bindings are possible optimisations rather than implemented guarantees. MRTK's proximity and hover lights inform that latter design.
